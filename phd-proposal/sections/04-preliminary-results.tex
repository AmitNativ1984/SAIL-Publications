\section{Preliminary Results}
\label{sec:prelim_results}

\subsection{Overview}
\label{subsec:prelim_overview}
Fully neuromorphic agile flight requires three parts: event-based sensing, a spiking neural network (SNN) that maps the events to low-level control commands, and neuromorphic hardware that runs it. The first stage of this research isolates the second part, training the SNN controller. We therefore keep the perception conventional: the policy observes a depth image, compressed by a frozen encoder, together with the drone's state, and outputs attitude commands that fly the drone through clutter it has never seen. Replacing the depth image with events is part of later stages of the research.

Training a spiking policy for this task raised two problems, each discussed in Section~\ref{sec:related_work} and addressed here:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RL from scratch stalls.} A population-coded spiking actor (PopSAN) trained with PPO from scratch does not progress through the curriculum, while an ANN with the same reward does (Section~\ref{subsec:scratch}). We therefore train an ANN teacher first and distil it into the SNN student, following proximal policy distillation (Section~\ref{subsec:distill}).
    \item \textbf{Encoding continuous observations into spikes.} The observations are continuous, so they must be encoded into spikes, here by populations of neurons with Gaussian receptive fields. The encoding must keep every neuron active: a neuron whose receptive field lies where the observation never goes never fires and carries no information. We set the encoder ranges from the teacher's observation distribution, which leaves almost no neuron silent (Section~\ref{subsec:bounds}).
\end{itemize}
The section first defines the problem (Section~\ref{subsec:problem}), then reports the training of each component (Section~\ref{subsec:training_results}), and finally the resulting student: its encoders, spiking activity and estimated energy (Section~\ref{subsec:final_results}).

\subsection{Training Results}
\label{subsec:training_results}

\paragraph{Environment.} All policies are trained and evaluated in the Aerial Gym Simulator~\cite{kulkarniAerialGymSimulator2025a}, which simulates thousands of aerial robots in parallel on the GPU, with efficient ray casting for onboard depth cameras, and its built-in attitude controller~\cite{leeControlComplexManeuvers2011}. The simulated vehicle is a digital twin of the lab's F450 quadrotor. The drone flies inside a bounded volume of $20 \times 20 \times [4, 6]$\,m that contains static obstacles (trees, panels, spheres, cylinders and perimeter walls), placed by a Poisson process. Every episode samples a new obstacle set together with a start and a goal, so the policy is always evaluated on environments it has not seen. The obstacle density grows linearly over a curriculum of 30 levels, up to 0.08 obstacles/m$^3$ at level 30. The level rises by one when the success rate exceeds 70\% and drops by one when it falls below 60\%. Every command is delayed by a latency drawn from the measured capture-to-control latency of the onboard pipeline ($45 \pm 10$\,ms), and the policy runs at the measured control rate of $\sim$33\,Hz.

\paragraph{Sensing.} The goal direction, distance and velocity come from a simulated state estimator rather than the true state: its position estimate has a per-episode offset ($\sigma = 0.05$\,m) that drifts as a random walk ($0.02$\,m/$\sqrt{\mathrm{s}}$), its velocity has white noise ($\sigma = 0.05$\,m/s), and its yaw has a per-episode bias ($\sigma = 0.05$\,rad), which rotates both the goal direction and the velocity. The reward is computed from the true state. The depth latent $z_t \in \mathbb{R}^{32}$ encodes a $320 \times 180$ depth image from a forward-facing camera modelled on the Intel RealSense D435 ($87^\circ \times 56^\circ$ field of view, 0.1--10\,m range). Before entering the policy, each of the 49 dimensions is standardised with a running mean and variance estimated from the training observations, and clipped to $\pm 5$; the statistics are frozen at deployment, and the student starts from the teacher's.

\paragraph{Episodes.} Every episode starts from a new start position $\mathbf{p}_s$, goal $\mathbf{p}_g$ and obstacle set, and ends at the first step at which one of the following holds:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Success:} the drone is within $0.4$\,m of the goal;
    \item \textbf{Crash:} the drone collides with an obstacle or leaves the flight volume;
    \item \textbf{Timeout:} 800 control steps (24\,s) have passed.
\end{itemize}
The objective is to maximise the probability of success over the distribution of environments, with time to goal as a secondary criterion, so that a policy that hovers safely does not count as a solution.

Unless stated otherwise, the success and crash rates below are measured over the training episodes, in which the actions are sampled from the stochastic policy. Deterministic evaluations of the final policies are reported in Section~\ref{subsec:final_results}.

\subsubsection{Deep Collision Encoding}
\label{subsec:vae}
We collected 85,000 depth images in the training environments, with obstacles placed by the task's Poisson process at different densities. The simulated RealSense D435 camera ($320 \times 180$ pixels, $87^\circ$ horizontal field of view, 0.1--10\,m range) was placed at random positions, with roll and pitch up to $\pm 45^\circ$ and uniform yaw, to cover the attitudes seen in flight. Frames with fewer than 5\% valid (in-range) pixels were discarded. For each image, the collision image was precomputed by inflating the obstacles by the drone's radius (Section~\ref{subsec:dce}). Depth $\tilde{d}$ is inverse-normalised as $d = 1 - \tilde{d}/d_{\max}$, with $d_{\max} = 7$\,m, so that near obstacles have large values. A convolutional VAE with a 32-dimensional latent is trained with a reconstruction loss that weights near obstacles more,
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N} (1+\alpha d_n)(\hat{d}_n-d_n)^2 ,
    \label{eq:recon_loss}
\end{equation}
where $d_n$ is pixel $n$ of the collision image, $\hat{d}_n$ its reconstruction and $\alpha = 5$. The full loss is $\mathcal{L} = \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{recon}} + \beta_{\mathrm{KL}} \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}$ with $\beta_{\mathrm{KL}} = 0.001$.

A first dataset of 43,000 images was rendered in a stand-alone environment that had drifted from the navigation task: it had no ground plane, lacked some obstacle classes, and used a $1280 \times 720$ camera downsampled offline. Re-collecting the data in the navigation environment itself, with the D435 camera model rendered natively at $320 \times 180$, reduced the validation loss from 0.0175 to 0.0125 ($-28\%$). \autoref{fig:DCE} shows example reconstructions.

\subsubsection{ANN Teacher}
\label{subsec:teacher}
The teacher is a multilayer perceptron actor--critic with separate actor and critic networks (hidden layers 256--256--64, ELU), trained with PPO on 1024 parallel environments. It was trained in two stages (\autoref{fig:teacher_training}). From scratch, with the reward of Section~\ref{subsec:reward}, it climbed the curriculum steadily and reached the top level, 30, after 790 PPO epochs ($1.0 \times 10^8$ environment steps). It was then fine-tuned at level 30 with the measured capture-to-control latency added to the simulation. At level 30 it succeeds in 85.3\% of the training episodes and crashes in 14.2\%. This teacher is the reference the SNN student must match.

\subsubsection{PopSAN Trained from Scratch}
\label{subsec:scratch}
The student is a population-coded spiking actor network (PopSAN)~\cite{tangDeepReinforcementLearning2020} (Section~\ref{sec:related_work}, \autoref{fig:popsan_encoding}). Each of the 49 observation dimensions is encoded by a population of 10 neurons (490 in total), feeding two hidden layers of 256 current-based LIF neurons (synaptic decay $\alpha = 0.5$, membrane decay $\beta = 0.75$, threshold 0.5), simulated for $T = 5$ time steps per control step. Each of the 4 action dimensions is decoded from the firing rates of its own population of 10 output neurons. The critic is an ANN, used only during training.

Trained with PPO from scratch, PopSAN does not progress through the curriculum. \autoref{fig:scratch_curriculum} compares six PopSAN runs with five ANN runs, trained on an earlier version of the task with the same environment and reward for both networks. Four of the five ANN runs reach the top level of that curriculum (25); the best PopSAN run stalls at level 14, and four of the six never leave level 0. Since the ANN learns the task and the SNN does not, the difficulty lies in training the spiking network, not in the task. This motivates training the ANN first and distilling it into the SNN.

Four of the five ANN runs reach the top level; the best PopSAN run stalls at level 14 and four never leave level 0.}

\subsubsection{Teacher--Student Distillation}
\label{subsec:distill}
The student is trained with proximal policy distillation~\eqref{eq:ppd_background}~\cite{spiglerProximalPolicyDistillation2025}: it collects its own rollouts and minimises the PPO loss plus the KL divergence between the teacher's and the student's Gaussian action distributions, weighted by $\lambda_{\mathrm{KD}}$. Training runs at curriculum level 30 with the teacher's reward, and the student's critic is initialised from the teacher's critic. The teacher term is annealed out as the student improves. $\lambda_{\mathrm{KD}}$ starts at 0.5 and is reduced by 5\% of its initial value at every curriculum check (every 16 rollouts) in which the student succeeds in at least half of its episodes. Guided by the teacher, the student learns quickly: its success rate reaches 75\% after 100 epochs and 83\% after 200, where PPO from scratch stalled at the lowest levels.

The training was unstable once the teacher was released (\autoref{fig:distill_stability}). With PPO's adaptive learning-rate schedule, which lowers the learning rate when the policy changes too much in an update and raises it otherwise, the student matched the teacher (85\%) while the teacher term was active. Once $\lambda_{\mathrm{KD}}$ reached zero, the schedule raised the learning rate from $\sim 10^{-5}$ to its ceiling of $10^{-3}$, the policy entropy ran away, and the success rate fell to 57\%. With a constant learning rate of $10^{-4}$ the student holds a success rate of 79--84\% after the release, up to epoch 2000, against the teacher's 85\%. Over its last 50 epochs it succeeds in 81.1\% of the episodes and crashes in 18.7\%. The entropy still drifts upwards after the release, more slowly; this growth of the action variance is under investigation.\footnote{The adaptive-learning-rate run used an earlier variant of the field-of-view term, which penalised only horizontal velocity away from the camera axis; the constant-learning-rate run used the teacher's reward.}

\subsubsection{Population Encoder Bounds}
\label{subsec:bounds}
Each encoder neuron $j$ of observation dimension $i$ has a Gaussian receptive field with mean $\mu_{ij}$ and width $\sigma_{ij}$, and receives the constant input current
\begin{equation}
    A_{ij} = \exp\!\left(-\frac{(o_i - \mu_{ij})^2}{2\sigma_{ij}^2}\right), \qquad o_i \in [o_i^{\min}, o_i^{\max}],
    \label{eq:grf}
\end{equation}
which drives a non-leaky integrate-and-fire neuron with threshold 0.95 and reset by subtraction for $T = 5$ steps. The neuron fires within the window only if $5A_{ij} \geq 0.95$, i.e.\ $A_{ij} \geq 0.19$. A neuron whose field lies where the observation never goes is therefore silent and carries no information. The observation range $[o_i^{\min}, o_i^{\max}]$ decides where the fields are placed, and observations outside it are clipped.

The ranges are taken from the teacher. The trained teacher flew 200 episodes at curriculum level 30, and for every observation dimension we recorded the 1st and 99th percentiles of its observations, after the same normalisation the policy applies (Section~\ref{subsec:training_results}). These percentiles are the bounds $[o_i^{\min}, o_i^{\max}]$. A symmetric range around the mean, as used for input normalisation, misplaces the fields on the many skewed or one-sided dimensions, such as the distance to the goal or several depth latents. Percentiles of the teacher's own flights cover the states the student will visit, since distillation keeps the student close to the teacher.

Within each range, the 10 means are spaced evenly with spacing $\Delta_i = (o_i^{\max} - o_i^{\min})/9$, and the widths are initialised to $\sigma_{ij} = 0.75\,\Delta_i$, so that neighbouring fields overlap. A neuron then fires for any observation within $\sigma_{ij}\sqrt{2\ln(1/0.19)} = 1.37\,\Delta_i$ of its mean, so every value inside the range drives at least two neurons. Means and widths are learned during training.

\autoref{fig:encoder_bounds} compares the two choices of range on two skewed dimensions. With a symmetric range of $\pm 3$ standard deviations, the fields beyond the observed values never reach the firing threshold: on the observations of the student's own flights at level 30, 16 of the 490 encoder neurons are silent, all of them in the 17 state dimensions. With the percentile ranges, only one neuron is silent.

\subsection{Final Results}
\label{subsec:final_results}

The final student is the constant-learning-rate run of Section~\ref{subsec:distill}, taken at epoch 950. We evaluate it and the ANN teacher in the same way: the deterministic policy (the action mean) flies 128 parallel environments for 4000 control steps at a fixed curriculum level, with a new obstacle set in every episode, which gives about 3,300 episodes per level. \autoref{tab:student_teacher} shows that the spiking student nearly matches its teacher. At every level its success rate is within 1.2 percentage points of the teacher's, and in the densest clutter, level 30, it succeeds in 84.9\% of the episodes against the teacher's 86.1\%. Almost all failures are collisions; neither policy times out.

\subsubsection{Trained Encoders and Spiking Activity}
\label{subsec:spikes}
The spiking activity below is measured on the student flying at curriculum level 30 (128 environments, 2000 control steps, 1670 episodes).

\paragraph{Encoder.} Training moves the receptive fields only slightly from their initial placement (\autoref{fig:encoder_learned}). Where the observations concentrate, as at the upper end of the goal direction, the fields narrow and crowd together, which gives finer resolution where the policy needs it. One encoder neuron of 490 remains silent.

    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{encoder_learned.png}

Gray: histogram of the normalised observation during flights at level 30; dashed lines: the percentile range. The fields of the goal direction narrow towards its frequently visited upper end; those of gravity stay close to their initial placement.}
    \label{fig:encoder_learned}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Firing rates.} \autoref{fig:spike_rates} shows, for every layer, the distribution of the firing rate: the fraction of the $T = 5$ time steps in which a neuron spikes, averaged over all control steps. The network is sparse. The encoder fires at 16.9\% on average, the first hidden layer at 20.0\% and the second at 31.3\%, and only one neuron in each of the encoder and the second hidden layer is silent. No neuron fires at every time step, so no layer is saturated, and the activity is spread over the neurons rather than carried by a few.

\subsubsection{Energy Estimate}
\label{subsec:energy}
We estimate the inference energy per control step from operation counts, as described in Section~\ref{sec:related_work} (Table~\ref{tab:op_energy}). For the ANN teacher's actor (49--256--256--64--4), every connection costs one MAC, \eqref{eq:energy_ann_neuron}: $49 \cdot 256 + 256 \cdot 256 + 256 \cdot 64 + 64 \cdot 4 = 94{,}720$ MACs, or $94{,}720 \times 4.6\,\mathrm{pJ} = 435.7$\,nJ per step. For the SNN student, every spike that crosses a synapse costs one AC, \eqref{eq:energy_snn_neuron}, counted from the measured spikes of each layer over the $T = 5$ time steps; every neuron update costs one MC; and the Gaussian receptive fields and the output decoder, which run once per control step, cost one MAC per operation. The depth encoder is the same ANN for both policies and is excluded from the comparison. \autoref{tab:energy} summarises the result: the student needs 190.7\,nJ per control step, $2.3\times$ less than the teacher.

    \label{tab:energy}

        \hline
        Policy & Operation & Count & Energy (nJ) \\
        \hline
        ANN teacher & MAC & 94,720 & 435.7 \\
        \hline
        SNN student & synaptic AC & 187,742 & 169.0 \\
                    & neuron update (MC) & 5,210 & 19.3 \\

The saving comes from the hidden layers and is partly lost at the input. Between the two hidden layers, the student performs 65,507 ACs (59.0\,nJ) where the teacher performs 65,536 MACs (301.5\,nJ), a $5.1\times$ saving. At the input, however, population coding multiplies the 49 observations into 490 encoder neurons, which produce 106,233 ACs (95.6\,nJ), 57\% of all synaptic operations, against the teacher's 12,544 MACs (57.7\,nJ). The input encoding is therefore the main lever for reducing the energy further, for example with fewer neurons per population or sparser encoder firing.
