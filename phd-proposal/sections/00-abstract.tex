\section*{Abstract}
The faster a drone flies through cluttered environments, the less time it has between seeing an obstacle and reacting to it, so high-speed flight requires low perception and control latency. On a small drone, this latency is limited by two parts of the loop: the sensor, which captures the scene only as fast as its frame rate allows, and the onboard computer, which must process each measurement in time. A faster computer, however, is heavier and draws more energy, which shortens the flight and reduces agility. Neuromorphic computing offers a promising way around both limits. Inspired by biological nervous systems, it represents information as sparse spikes and computes only when they arrive, which makes it more energy-efficient than conventional deep learning. To take full advantage of it, neuromorphic computation must be coupled with event-based sensing, so that spiking neural networks (SNNs) operate directly on the sparse data the sensor produces.

This research aims towards fully end-to-end neuromorphic high-speed flight. A central challenge is training the SNN. Its spikes are not differentiable, and when it is trained with reinforcement learning from scratch for navigation through unseen clutter, it stalls early in the curriculum while a conventional artificial neural network (ANN) with the same reward does not. Continuous observations must also be encoded into spikes without leaving neurons silent. Preliminary results in simulation, with depth perception, address both. We distil a trained ANN teacher into a population-coded SNN student, and place the student's input receptive fields from the teacher's observation distribution so that almost no encoder neuron is silent. In the densest clutter, the student succeeds in 84.9\% of the flights against the teacher's 86.1\%, at an estimated $2.3\times$ lower inference energy.

The research then works towards closing the remaining gap to fully neuromorphic agile flight, starting with the sensor. Event cameras are neuromorphic vision sensors. Each pixel independently and asynchronously reports changes in brightness, which gives microsecond latency, a high dynamic range (about 140\,dB against 60\,dB for frame cameras), very little motion blur and low power consumption. Their output is sparse, event-driven spikes, which match the computation of an SNN without converting them into dense frames. Future work will replace the depth input with events and train the full event-to-command policy, deploy it on a neuromorphic processor and fly it on a real quadrotor, and compare it with a matched ANN in flight performance, latency and energy. It will also study whether the internal state of the SNN can act as a memory when the onboard sensors observe only part of the state. The expected contribution is a method to train and deploy end-to-end neuromorphic policies for agile flight from onboard vision, and a measured assessment of what they gain over ANNs.
