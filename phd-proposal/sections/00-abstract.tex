\section*{Abstract}
How fast a small unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) can safely fly through unknown clutter depends on how quickly it perceives obstacles and reacts to them, and on a small drone this latency is limited by the onboard compute and energy budget. Learned policies have flown quadrotors at high speed, but they rely on frame-based cameras and dense artificial neural networks (ANNs) on GPU-class computers. Event cameras report brightness changes asynchronously with microsecond latency, and spiking neural networks (SNNs) on neuromorphic processors compute only when spikes arrive. Together they promise fast, low-power perception and control, yet neuromorphic flight has so far been shown only for slow maneuvers or with the state measured externally.

This research works towards an end-to-end neuromorphic flight controller for agile flight through unknown clutter: an event camera feeds an SNN policy that runs on a neuromorphic processor onboard and outputs high-rate low-level commands. It pursues two objectives. The first is to develop this controller, train it in simulation, transfer it to real flight, and compare it with a matched ANN policy in flight performance, latency and energy consumption. The second is to investigate whether the internal state of an SNN can act as a memory when the onboard sensors observe only part of the state, for example when flying without velocity or position information.

Preliminary results in simulation address the training of the spiking policy, with conventional depth perception. A spiking actor trained with reinforcement learning from scratch stalls in the curriculum while an ANN with the same reward does not. We therefore distil a trained ANN teacher into a population-coded SNN student, and place the student's input receptive fields from the teacher's observation distribution so that almost no encoder neuron is silent. In the densest clutter, the student succeeds in 84.9\% of the flights against the teacher's 86.1\%, at an estimated $2.3\times$ lower inference energy.

Future work will replace the depth input with events and train the full event-to-command pipeline, deploy the policy on neuromorphic hardware and fly it on a real quadrotor, measure its latency and energy against the ANN, reduce the cost of the input encoding, and study stateful spiking policies under partial observability. The expected contribution is a training and deployment method for neuromorphic agile flight from onboard vision, and an experimental assessment of what spiking policies gain over ANNs in latency, energy and memory.
