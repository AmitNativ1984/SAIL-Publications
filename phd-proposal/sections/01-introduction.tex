\section{Introduction}
Small unmanned aerial vehicles (UAVs) that can fly fast through unknown, cluttered environments, such as forests, collapsed buildings or warehouses, would make search and rescue, inspection and delivery faster and safer. The faster a drone flies, however, the less time it has between seeing an obstacle and reacting to it. Falanga et al.~\cite{falangaHowFastToo2019} showed that the maximum safe speed through clutter is bounded jointly by the perception latency, the sensing range and the actuation limits of the vehicle. On a small drone, latency is in turn tied to the onboard compute and energy budget: a heavier, more power-hungry computer reacts faster but shortens the flight and reduces agility. This research asks how a small UAV can perceive and decide fast enough for agile flight within this budget.

The established approach divides autonomy into a modular pipeline of state estimation, mapping, planning and control. Such pipelines now fly palm-sized drone swarms through dense forests with only onboard sensing~\cite{zhouSwarmMicroFlying2022}, but each stage adds latency and its errors propagate to the next. Learned policies instead map sensor observations directly to commands. Loquercio et al.~\cite{loquercioLearningHighSpeedFlight2021} trained a policy in simulation by imitating a privileged expert and flew it at high speed through unseen forests, outperforming modular baselines. Song et al.~\cite{songLearningPerceptionAwareAgile2023} trained a reinforcement learning (RL) teacher with privileged state and distilled it into a vision-based student. Kaufmann et al.~\cite{kaufmannChampionlevelDroneRacing2023} presented Swift, an RL-trained policy that beat human world champions in drone racing, winning 15 of 25 races at speeds of about 22\,m/s. These systems rely on frame-based cameras and GPU-class onboard computers~\cite{foehnAgiliciousOpensourceOpenhardware2022}, so their reaction time is bounded by the camera frame rate and their energy cost by dense neural network inference.

Event cameras address the sensing side of this limit. Each pixel reports brightness changes asynchronously, with microsecond resolution, high dynamic range and little motion blur~\cite{gallegoEventbasedVisionSurvey2022}. They have enabled the avoidance of fast-moving obstacles with 3.5\,ms latency~\cite{falangaDynamicObstacleAvoidance2020} and learned obstacle avoidance at up to 9.8\,m/s~\cite{bhattacharyaMonocularEventBasedVision2024,messikommerApproximateImitationLearning}. These learned policies, however, accumulate events into dense frames and process them with conventional artificial neural networks (ANNs), discarding the sparsity and asynchrony that make the sensor attractive.

Spiking neural networks (SNNs) are the natural processor for this data. Their neurons communicate through sparse binary spikes, so on neuromorphic hardware they compute only when an input arrives~\cite{eshraghianTrainingSpikingNeural2023,muirRoadCommercialSuccess2025}, and their membrane potentials carry a decaying memory of past inputs. Fully neuromorphic flight has been demonstrated, but only in parts. Paredes-Vall\'es et al.~\cite{paredes-vallesFullyNeuromorphicVision2023} ran event-based ego-motion estimation on a Loihi chip at 200\,Hz and 27\,$\mu$J per inference, but for slow maneuvers above a flat, static floor. Stroobants et al.~\cite{stroobantsNeuromorphicAttitudeEstimation2025} mapped inertial measurements to motor commands with an SNN at 500\,Hz, also with gentle maneuvers. Van Breukelen Castillo et al.~\cite{vanbreukelencastilloSpikingNeuralNetworks2026} flew an RL-trained spiking policy through racing gates at about 8\,m/s, but from motion-capture state and with the network state reset at every step. No existing work closes the loop from onboard vision to agile control through unknown clutter with an SNN, and training SNNs with RL for such tasks remains difficult~\cite{tangDeepReinforcementLearning2020}.

This research works towards that missing system: an end-to-end neuromorphic flight controller in which an event camera feeds an SNN policy running on a neuromorphic processor, which outputs high-rate low-level commands for agile flight through unknown clutter. Since training the SNN directly is hard, we train an ANN teacher with RL in massively parallel simulation and distill it into a population-coded spiking student. We compare the result with a matched ANN in flight performance, latency and energy (Objective~1). We also ask whether the intrinsic state of the SNN can act as memory that improves control when the onboard sensors observe only part of the state (Objective~2). As a first step, we have trained a spiking policy, distilled from an ANN teacher, that navigates a simulated quadrotor through dense, unseen clutter from depth images (Section~\ref{sec:prelim_results}).
